Gardening While Falling: The Science of Space Botany

How do you grow a chili pepper when water won't pour and "down" has gone missing?

A quick heads-up: I'm a mathematician who got a bit bored and wanted to try something new. Please join in on the fun, at your own risk! 😉

Science keeps moving the boundaries of what seems possible. Astronauts aboard the International Space Station (ISS) have grown lettuce, radishes, tomatoes and chili peppers.3 Space botany is the study of how plants live and grow in space, and how we can help them do it.

1. First, why does everything float?

It is not because gravity has vanished. At the station's altitude, Earth's gravity is still about 90% as strong as on the ground.1 We can check this with Newton's inverse-square law. Take Earth's radius R ≈ 6,371 km and an altitude h ≈ 400 km:

g(h) / g(0) = [ R / (R + h) ]² = (6371 / 6771)² ≈ 0.89

The station and everything in it are in continuous free fall. They also move sideways so fast (about 28,000 km/h) that Earth's surface curves away as they fall, so they keep circling instead of landing.1 Since astronaut, plant and water droplet all fall together, they appear weightless. This condition is called microgravity. "Zero gravity" is a popular but misleading name.

Astronauts rehearse spacewalks underwater, where they can practice moving in three dimensions, handling tools and keeping their orientation. It is a useful imitation, but not the same physics: in water, buoyancy balances your weight, while in orbit you are falling.

A little farther from home: what changes on the Moon?

Here is another way to picture gravity. Imagine the same 70 kg astronaut in three places: near Earth, aboard the ISS, and standing on the Moon. The astronaut's mass stays 70 kg throughout the journey. What changes is the gravitational force on them—and, when they stand on a surface, their weight.

At the Moon's surface, gravity is about 1.62 m/s², roughly one-sixth of the familiar 9.81 m/s² at Earth's surface. A 70 kg astronaut would still have a mass of 70 kg, but their gravitational weight would be much smaller.

On Earth686.7 N70 × 9.81 m/s²
At ISS altitude*about 607 N70 × 8.67 m/s²
On the Moon113.4 N70 × 1.62 m/s²

*The ISS-altitude figure is the gravitational force that would act on a supported 70 kg object at about 400 km altitude. Astronauts aboard the orbiting station do not feel that as ordinary weight because they and the station are falling together.

Mass is not weight

Mass describes how much matter an object contains and is measured in kilograms (kg). Weight is the force of gravity on that mass and is measured in newtons (N). The Moon does not make an astronaut's mass smaller; it makes their weight smaller. Their inertia—the resistance to changes in motion—remains much like it was on Earth.

Important distinction for beginners

Microgravity (ISS) is not the same as low gravity (Moon). On the station, objects do not fall “down” relative to the cabin; they float. On the Moon there is still a clear downward direction—just a much gentler one. Plants, water and astronauts therefore behave differently in each place.

Why does a jump look different on the Moon?

Imagine releasing a ball from rest at a height of 2 meters, ignoring air resistance. On Earth it takes about 0.64 seconds to fall; on the Moon it takes about 1.57 seconds. The ball falls more slowly because lunar gravity gives it less downward acceleration. An astronaut can therefore make longer, higher-looking hops, although their spacesuit and the inertia of their body still take effort to move.

t = √(2d/g)   →   Earth: 0.64 s   |   Moon: 1.57 s

Think of a soft elevator that suddenly starts falling freely: you float inside it. That is roughly the ISS experience. On the Moon you are still standing on a solid floor, but the floor only pulls you with one-sixth the familiar strength—so jumps last longer and land more gently.

And what about the trip itself? The astronaut does not suddenly feel lunar gravity increasing as a heavy pull while floating inside the spacecraft. During a carefully planned coast, the spacecraft and astronaut are in free fall together. Near the Moon, lunar gravity shapes the spacecraft's path; once the astronaut lands and is supported by the surface, they feel their reduced lunar weight.

This is the useful distinction for our space-gardening story: microgravity aboard an orbiting spacecraft is not the same as the Moon's weaker surface gravity. On the ISS, plants, water and crew float together in free fall. On the Moon, objects still fall toward the ground—just with a gentler acceleration than on Earth.

How the three environments affect a garden

The practical differences matter a great deal when you try to grow food. Here is a quick side-by-side summary:

Feature Earth surface ISS (microgravity) Moon surface (~1/6 g)
Preferred “down” Clear and strong None – everything floats Clear but weak
Water behavior Pours, drains, forms streams Clings, forms floating blobs Still drains, but more slowly; surface tension more noticeable
Root orientation (gravitropism) Roots grow downward reliably Gravity cue almost absent; plants rely more on light Roots still sense “down”, but the signal is weaker
Air movement Warm air rises naturally No natural convection – fans required Weaker convection; some forced airflow still helpful
Soil / medium Ordinary soil works Loose soil floats away; special pillows or hydroponics needed Soil stays put, but water management must be careful
What a gardener feels Normal weight Weightless (free-fall) About one-sixth normal weight

On the ISS

Because there is no “down”, water must be delivered by wicks or carefully controlled systems. Roots cannot use gravity to find the right direction, so light (and sometimes mechanical cues) become the main guides. Fans replace the missing breeze.

On the Moon

A gentle downward pull still exists, so conventional drainage and root growth are possible in principle. The slower fall of water and the reduced convection of air, however, mean that over-watering or stagnant humidity can still become problems. Future lunar greenhouses will need their own engineering solutions.

Why this matters for the rest of the article

When you read about Veggie’s clay pillows, LED lighting and fans, remember that those inventions were designed for the floating world of the ISS. A garden on the Moon would face a different (though still challenging) set of physics. Understanding the distinction helps us appreciate both the cleverness of today’s space gardens and the new questions that will arise when humans try to farm on other worlds.

2. Why ordinary garden soil is a problem

  • Loose particles drift. Dust and grit could reach eyes, equipment and air filters.
  • Water does not drain. Without a useful "downward" pull, surface tension dominates. Water clings to surfaces and gathers into blobs.
  • Roots need oxygen. Too much water around a root suffocates it; too little leaves it dry.2
  • Soil is alive. Many of its bacteria and fungi are helpful, but uncontrolled microbes are a concern in a closed cabin.

So the challenge is not that plants cannot grow in space. It is delivering water, air and nutrients in the right balance.

3. The clay that isn't playdough

A substrate is whatever roots grow in. When I first read "clay-based substrate", I pictured the squishy stuff toddlers play with. The space version is quite different: calcined clay, clay heated strongly until it becomes hard, porous granules. It is similar to material used on baseball fields.4

Its pores and the gaps between granules help share water and air around the roots. Water moves through small spaces by capillary action, the same effect that draws a spill into a paper towel, and it works without gravity's help.

4. Meet Veggie, a space garden

NASA's Vegetable Production System, Veggie, is about the size of carry-on luggage and typically holds six plants. Each grows in a fabric "pillow" filled with clay-based growing medium and fertilizer.2 The fertilizer is released slowly, and water wicks into the pillows from a reservoir.5 Astronauts still tend the plants and add water.4

Light replaces the Sun, and helps show the way

On Earth, roots tend to grow downward in response to gravity, a behavior called gravitropism. In orbit that cue is largely missing, and plants rely on others, such as light, to orient their growth.2 LEDs supply mostly the red and blue light that plants use heavily, so the chamber often glows magenta.2 Green LEDs are included too,4 and help the plants look more natural to the crew.

Fans replace the breeze

In microgravity, warm air does not rise as it does on Earth. Fans keep air moving so leaves get fresh carbon dioxide and moisture does not linger around the plants.

6. A short harvest diary

  • 2015: The crew grew lettuce in Veggie and got to eat it.3
  • 2016: Astronaut Scott Kelly nursed struggling zinnias back to health, and they flowered.2
  • 2021: Chile peppers (my favorite!) grew for 137 days in the Advanced Plant Habitat. The crew enjoyed some in tacos.7
  • 2022–2023: Dwarf tomatoes grew in Veggie. Watering difficulties and low humidity limited the harvest to 12 ripe fruits, all returned to Earth for study.8
  • October 2026: A study called MVP Plant-02 arrived to examine how plants' genes respond to microgravity.9

Other crops include Chinese cabbage, mizuna mustard, kale and radishes.2,3

7. And the fish?

I joked to myself about bringing fish along, but scientists got there first! Japanese rice fish, called medaka, mated aboard the space shuttle Columbia in 1994, and their eggs developed and hatched. In 2012, medaka lived for two months in an aquarium on the ISS, helping researchers study bone changes in microgravity.10 Chickens are still waiting for their invitation. 🐔

Why it matters

Missions far from Earth cannot depend on frequent grocery deliveries, and some vitamins in packaged food break down over time. Fresh crops could help keep crews healthy, and gardening may lift their spirits.2 Space botany does not recreate Earth exactly. It identifies what a plant needs (light, water, air, nutrients and a way to orient) and engineers each one. As a mathematician, I find that delightful: change one condition, and a whole familiar system has to be solved again. 🚀🌱
