Nonnegative Matrix Factorization

A mathematical perspective: from the least-squares objective to the multiplicative update rules, with a proof that the error never goes up and a demo you can run in the page.

By Kristina P. Sinaga Updated 4 November 2026 About 20 minutes to read
On this page

    Key ideas

    • NMF writes a nonnegative matrix as $\mathbf{X}\approx\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}$ with $\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}\ge 0$. Columns of $\mathbf{U}$ are parts, and columns of $\mathbf{V}$ say how much of each part a sample uses.
    • The least-squares objective is not jointly convex, but it is convex in $\mathbf{U}$ alone and in $\mathbf{V}$ alone, so we alternate between the two.
    • The multiplicative updates are gradient descent with an entrywise step size chosen so that nonnegativity is automatic.
    • An auxiliary-function argument proves the error never increases. That guarantees descent, not a global optimum.
    • Squared error is sensitive to outliers, which motivates robust variants.

    Why nonnegative factors?

    Nonnegative matrix factorization (NMF) approximates a nonnegative data matrix by the product of two smaller nonnegative matrices. One factor holds basis vectors; the other holds the coefficients that combine them. Because no entry may be negative, the factors can only add up and never cancel one another. The basis vectors therefore tend to be recognizable parts, such as strokes of a digit, features of a face or the vocabulary of a topic, rather than abstract holistic patterns [1].

    That interpretability, together with a simple algorithm, explains why NMF is used for face recognition, document analysis, hyperspectral imaging and recommender systems. This post sets up the model, states the classic multiplicative update rules [2], derives them step by step, proves that they never increase the error, and lets you watch them run. You should be comfortable with matrix products, the trace, and gradients of scalar functions of matrices.

    Notation

    Matrices, vectors
    Matrices are bold capitals ($\mathbf{A}$), vectors bold lowercase ($\mathbf{a}$). Entry $(i,k)$ of $\mathbf{A}$ is $A_{ik}$.
    Rows, columns
    The $i$-th row of $\mathbf{A}$ is $\mathbf{a}_{i,:}$ and the $i$-th column is $\mathbf{a}_i$.
    Transpose, trace
    $\mathbf{A}^{\top}$ and $\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{A})$. The identity matrix is $\mathbf{I}$.
    Norms
    For $\rho>0$, $\|\mathbf{a}\|_{\rho}=\big(\sum_{k}|a_k|^{\rho}\big)^{1/\rho}$ (a true norm when $\rho\ge 1$). The Frobenius norm is $\|\mathbf{A}\|_F=\sqrt{\sum_{i,k}A_{ik}^2}=\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})}$.
    Inner product
    $\langle\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{B})=\sum_{i,k}A_{ik}B_{ik}$, so $\|\mathbf{A}\|_F^2=\langle\mathbf{A},\mathbf{A}\rangle$.
    Entrywise operations
    $\odot$ is the entrywise (Hadamard) product. A fraction between two matrices of the same size, such as $\mathbf{A}/\mathbf{B}$, is entrywise division. Ordinary products like $\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\top}$ are matrix products.

    The model

    Let $\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_n]\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^{d\times n}$ hold $n$ samples, each with $d$ nonnegative features. For a chosen inner dimension $c$ (the number of parts or clusters), NMF looks for

    $$\mathbf{X}\approx\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V},\qquad \mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^{d\times c},\;\; \mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^{c\times n}.$$

    Read column by column, $\mathbf{x}_j\approx\sum_{k=1}^{c}V_{kj}\,\mathbf{u}_k$: every sample is a nonnegative combination of the $c$ basis vectors $\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_c$, and column $\mathbf{v}_j$ of $\mathbf{V}$ lists the weights.

    Compression

    The factors store $(d+n)c$ numbers instead of $dn$, which is a saving whenever $c. This holds, for example, when $c\ll\min(d,n)$. NMF is therefore a compressed, parts-based summary of the data.

    Reading the factors as clusters

    The columns of $\mathbf{U}$ can be viewed as cluster centres, and the columns of $\mathbf{V}$ as soft cluster indicators: sample $j$ leans towards cluster $\arg\max_k V_{kj}$.

    A caution on the clustering view

    Without extra constraints this reading is soft and overlapping. Exact links to $k$-means and spectral clustering appear when orthogonality constraints are added [3][4].

    The optimization problem

    The standard way to measure how well $\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}$ matches $\mathbf{X}$ is the squared Frobenius distance:

    $$\min_{\mathbf{U}\ge0,\;\mathbf{V}\ge0}\; f(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})=\|\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}\|_F^2=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_j-\mathbf{U}\mathbf{v}_j\|_2^2 .$$

    Three facts shape every algorithm for this problem.

    • Not jointly convex. Take the scalar case $f(u,v)=(1-uv)^2$. Its Hessian at the origin is $\begin{pmatrix}0&-2\\-2&0\end{pmatrix}$, which is indefinite.
    • Convex in each block. With $\mathbf{V}$ fixed, $f$ is a convex quadratic in $\mathbf{U}$, and vice versa. This justifies alternating (block coordinate) minimization.
    • Hard in general. Exact NMF is NP-hard [7], so in practice we look for stationary points, not guaranteed global minima.

    Because the loss squares every residual, a few badly corrupted samples can dominate it. We return to this in the practical notes.

    The multiplicative update rules

    Theorem 1 (Lee and Seung)

    The distance $\|\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}\|_F^2$ is nonincreasing under the updates

    $$\mathbf{U}\leftarrow\mathbf{U}\odot\frac{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{V}^{\top}}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\top}},\qquad \mathbf{V}\leftarrow\mathbf{V}\odot\frac{\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}},$$

    applied alternately, and the distance is invariant under them if and only if $\mathbf{U}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ are at a stationary point of the distance.

    Starting from positive $\mathbf{U}$ and $\mathbf{V}$, every entry is multiplied by a nonnegative ratio, so nonnegativity is preserved automatically. The ratio is above 1 where the entry should grow and below 1 where it should shrink.

    Fine print

    The proof below shows descent: the sequence $f(\mathbf{U}_t,\mathbf{V}_t)$ is nonincreasing and bounded below by 0, so it converges. That alone does not make the iterates converge to a stationary point. Also, an entry that is exactly zero stays zero forever, even when its gradient says it should grow, so a fixed point of the rule need not be a stationary point. Both issues are discussed in [5], which analyses a modified rule with convergence guarantees.

    Deriving the updates

    Step 1: expand the objective

    Using $\|\mathbf{A}\|_F^2=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})$ and the linearity of the trace,

    $$f=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})-\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V})-\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{X})+\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}).$$

    The two middle terms are transposes of each other and a trace equals the trace of its transpose, so they are equal. Hence

    $$f=\|\mathbf{X}\|_F^2-2\langle\mathbf{X},\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}\rangle+\langle\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V},\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}\rangle .$$

    Step 2: take gradients

    A compact route uses the identity $\langle\mathbf{A},\,d\mathbf{U}\,\mathbf{B}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{\top},\,d\mathbf{U}\rangle$. Differentiating with $\mathbf{V}$ fixed gives

    $$df=-2\langle\mathbf{X},d\mathbf{U}\,\mathbf{V}\rangle+2\langle\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V},d\mathbf{U}\,\mathbf{V}\rangle=2\langle(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}-\mathbf{X})\mathbf{V}^{\top},\,d\mathbf{U}\rangle,$$

    and in the same way for $\mathbf{V}$ with $\mathbf{U}$ fixed. Reading off the gradients:

    $$\nabla_{\mathbf{U}}f=-2\,\mathbf{X}\mathbf{V}^{\top}+2\,\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\top},$$
    $$\nabla_{\mathbf{V}}f=-2\,\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+2\,\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}.$$

    The same results follow from the trace rules $\nabla_{\mathbf{Z}}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z})=\mathbf{A}^{\top}$ and $\nabla_{\mathbf{Z}}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z}^{\top})=\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{A}^{\top})$ in the Matrix Cookbook [9].

    Step 3: plain gradient descent

    Alternating gradient steps with step sizes $\gamma_{\mathbf{U}},\gamma_{\mathbf{V}}$ give

    $$\mathbf{U}\leftarrow\mathbf{U}+\gamma_{\mathbf{U}}\odot\big(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{V}^{\top}-\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\big),\qquad \mathbf{V}\leftarrow\mathbf{V}+\gamma_{\mathbf{V}}\odot\big(\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}\big),$$

    where the factor 2 has been absorbed into the step sizes. With a scalar step size the subtraction can push entries below zero, which breaks the constraint. One could project back onto the nonnegative orthant, but there is a neater option.

    Step 4: choose an entrywise step size

    Let every entry have its own step size,

    $$\gamma_{\mathbf{U}}=\frac{\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\top}},\qquad \gamma_{\mathbf{V}}=\frac{\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}} .$$

    Substituting into , the term $\mathbf{U}\odot\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\top}/\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\top}$ cancels the $+\mathbf{U}$ and leaves exactly the rules of Theorem 1:

    $$\mathbf{U}+\frac{\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\top}}\odot\big(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{V}^{\top}-\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\big)=\mathbf{U}\odot\frac{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{V}^{\top}}{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\top}} .$$

    The subtraction has disappeared, so nothing can become negative.

    Step 5: the general recipe

    The same rule arises from splitting a gradient into its positive and negative parts. If $\nabla f=\nabla^{+}f-\nabla^{-}f$ with both parts entrywise nonnegative, then

    $$\theta\leftarrow\theta\odot\frac{\nabla^{-}f}{\nabla^{+}f}.$$

    For $\mathbf{U}$, $\nabla^{-}f=2\mathbf{X}\mathbf{V}^{\top}$ and $\nabla^{+}f=2\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\top}$, so reproduces the update. The recipe is a useful heuristic for other objectives, but it does not by itself prove descent. For this objective we prove it next.

    Why the error never increases

    Derivation explains where the rule comes from; it does not show that the error falls. The standard argument uses an auxiliary function (a majorizer). You can skip it on a first read.

    Proof of Theorem 1 (monotone descent) for readers who want the details

    Reduction. Since $f=\sum_j\|\mathbf{x}_j-\mathbf{U}\mathbf{v}_j\|^2$, the $\mathbf{V}$-update acts on each column separately. For the $\mathbf{U}$-update, use $\|\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}\|_F=\|\mathbf{X}^{\top}-\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\|_F$: the rows of $\mathbf{U}$ play the role of columns, with $\mathbf{V}^{\top}$ in place of $\mathbf{U}$. So it suffices to treat one vector problem. Let $\mathbf{W}\ge0$ be a fixed matrix, $\mathbf{x}\ge0$, $\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{W}$, and

    $$F(\mathbf{h})=\tfrac12\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{W}\mathbf{h}\|^2,\qquad \mathbf{h}_a\leftarrow \mathbf{h}_a\,\frac{(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x})_a}{(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{h})_a}.$$

    (The factor $\tfrac12$ changes neither minimizers nor monotonicity.) Assume $\mathbf{h}>0$ and $(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{h})_a>0$, which holds for positive initial values and no zero column in $\mathbf{W}$.

    Lemma 1 (auxiliary function). If $G(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{h}')\ge F(\mathbf{h})$ and $G(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{h})=F(\mathbf{h})$, then $F$ is nonincreasing under $\mathbf{h}^{t+1}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{h}}G(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{h}^{t})$, because

    $$F(\mathbf{h}^{t+1})\le G(\mathbf{h}^{t+1},\mathbf{h}^{t})\le G(\mathbf{h}^{t},\mathbf{h}^{t})=F(\mathbf{h}^{t}).$$

    Lemma 2. With $\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{h}^t)=\operatorname{Diag}\big((\mathbf{A}\mathbf{h}^t)_a/h^t_a\big)$, the function

    $$G(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{h}^t)=F(\mathbf{h}^t)+(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{h}^t)^{\top}\nabla F(\mathbf{h}^t)+\tfrac12(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{h}^t)^{\top}\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{h}^t)(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{h}^t)$$

    is an auxiliary function for $F$.

    Proof. $G(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{h})=F(\mathbf{h})$ is immediate. Since $F$ is quadratic, its Taylor expansion at $\mathbf{h}^t$ is exact with Hessian $\mathbf{A}$, so $G-F=\tfrac12(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{h}^t)^{\top}(\mathbf{K}-\mathbf{A})(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{h}^t)$. It remains to show $\mathbf{K}-\mathbf{A}\succeq0$. Let $\mathbf{M}_{ab}=h^t_a(\mathbf{K}-\mathbf{A})_{ab}h^t_b$; this is a congruence by a positive diagonal matrix, so it is PSD exactly when $\mathbf{K}-\mathbf{A}$ is. For any $\boldsymbol{\nu}$,

    $$\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\nu}=\sum_{a,b}A_{ab}h^t_ah^t_b\,\nu_a^2-\sum_{a,b}A_{ab}h^t_ah^t_b\,\nu_a\nu_b=\tfrac12\sum_{a,b}A_{ab}\,h^t_ah^t_b\,(\nu_a-\nu_b)^2\;\ge\;0,$$

    where the last step symmetrizes in $a,b$ and uses $A_{ab}\ge0$, which holds because $\mathbf{W}\ge0$. $\square$

    The minimizer is the update. Setting $\nabla_{\mathbf{h}}G=\nabla F(\mathbf{h}^t)+\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{h}^t)=\mathbf{0}$ with $\nabla F(\mathbf{h}^t)=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{h}^t-\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x}$ gives

    $$h_a=h^t_a-\frac{h^t_a}{(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{h}^t)_a}\big((\mathbf{A}\mathbf{h}^t)_a-(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x})_a\big)=h^t_a\,\frac{(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x})_a}{(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{h}^t)_a}.$$

    $G$ is strictly convex ($\mathbf{K}\succ0$), so this is its unique minimizer, and it is nonnegative, so it also satisfies the constraint. By Lemma 1, $F(\mathbf{h}^{t+1})\le F(\mathbf{h}^t)$. Applying this column by column to $\mathbf{V}$ and row by row to $\mathbf{U}$ proves the monotonicity claim. $\blacksquare$

    A minimal implementation

    The updates translate almost symbol for symbol into NumPy. A small constant in each denominator avoids division by zero.

    import numpy as np

def nmf_mu(X, c, n_iter=500, eps=1e-10, tol=1e-9, seed=0):
    """Multiplicative-update NMF for the objective ||X - U V||_F^2."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    d, n = X.shape
    U = rng.random((d, c)) + eps          # strictly positive start
    V = rng.random((c, n)) + eps
    losses = []
    for t in range(n_iter):
        U *= (X @ V.T) / (U @ V @ V.T + eps)
        V *= (U.T @ X) / (U.T @ U @ V + eps)
        losses.append(np.linalg.norm(X - U @ V, "fro") ** 2)
        if t > 0 and losses[-2] - losses[-1] <= tol * losses[0]:
            break                          # relative decrease is tiny
    return U, V, losses

    For real projects, sklearn.decomposition.NMF offers this scheme as solver="mu", along with other solvers and initializations.

    Practical notes and limitations

    • Initialize positive. Zeros are absorbing for multiplicative updates. Use strictly positive random starts, or a structured initialization such as one based on SVD.
    • Expect local solutions. The problem is nonconvex, so different starts reach different factorizations. Run several restarts and keep the best objective.
    • Factors are not unique. Columns of $\mathbf{U}$ can be permuted, and $\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D},\,\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{V}$ with a positive diagonal $\mathbf{D}$ gives the same product. Normalizing the columns of $\mathbf{U}$ removes the scale ambiguity.
    • Choose $c$ deliberately. Error always falls as $c$ grows, so look for an elbow, check stability across restarts, or validate on held-out entries.
    • Outliers matter. Squaring residuals lets a few corrupted samples dominate. Robust variants replace the squared loss by the unsquared $\ell_{2,1}$ norm, $\sum_j\|\mathbf{x}_j-\mathbf{U}\mathbf{v}_j\|_2$ [8].
    • Speed. Multiplicative updates are simple but can converge slowly. Projected gradient, coordinate descent and alternating nonnegative least squares are common faster alternatives [6][10].

    Check your understanding

    1. $\mathbf{X}$ is $100\times500$ and $c=10$. What are the shapes of $\mathbf{U}$ and $\mathbf{V}$, and how many numbers do the factors store compared with $\mathbf{X}$?

    Show answer
    The shapes are $100\times10$ and $10\times500$. The factors store $(100+500)\cdot10=6{,}000$ numbers against $50{,}000$ in $\mathbf{X}$, about 8 times fewer.

    2. Suppose $U_{ik}=0$ at initialization. What is its value after any number of multiplicative updates?

    Show answer
    It stays $0$, because each update multiplies the current value. This is why initial values should be strictly positive.

    3. Why not use the plain gradient step with one scalar step size?

    Show answer
    The subtraction in the step can make entries negative, violating the constraints. You would need a projection, or an entrywise step size as in Step 4, which turns the update into a product of nonnegative terms.

    4. Does Theorem 1 guarantee that the algorithm finds the global minimum?

    Show answer
    No. It guarantees only that the objective never increases. The problem is nonconvex and NP-hard in general, and even convergence to a stationary point needs extra arguments or a modified rule.
